# How a pension fund actually retains counsel

A public or corporate pension retains counsel through a process that is slower, more documented, and more conflict-sensitive than a company buying a deal lawyer. The general counsel or a named partner usually owns the relationship; the CIO owns the commercial problem; the board owns the fiduciary residue. This explainer is for both sides of that table. It is not legal advice and not a recommendation of any firm.

## The buying centre is not the deal team

On a private-markets commitment, the investment team can love a firm that has closed a hundred funds. The GC may still refuse them because they represent the manager. The board may still want a name it can defend in a public-records request.

If you are a partner trying to “get in front of CIOs,” ask who holds the panel. If you are a CIO trying to move fast, ask what the panel *cannot* do — then budget time.

## What “panel” usually means

Many large plans keep a pre-approved list: fiduciary/governance, investments, litigation, employment, real assets. Getting on it is procurement. Staying on it is matter-by-matter conflicts and bills that look like they were written for a legislature.

Smaller corporate plans and many family-adjacent pensions do not have a panel. They have a trusted partner and a fear of switching. That is a different sale.

## Documents that matter more than the pitch

- The last three similar opinions (redacted)
- The conflicts system, in writing
- Who signs, and who actually drafts
- How they handle a newspaper
