# The allocation nobody voted on

On Monday the Bank for International Settlements published the clearest official account yet of how the digital build-out was financed — and told a great many pension funds something about their own balance sheets that their own managers had not.

The paper is *Financing the digital economy: the role of private credit*, by Puriya Abbassi, Iñaki Aldasoro and Sebastian Doerr, in the September Quarterly Review. Its finding is a share, not a forecast. Private credit financed the post-2020 shift to the digital economy by lending against cash flows and intangible assets. A market bought for diversification acquired a sector.

**The headline is large enough to print.** Tech firms’ borrowing in this market rose from about $22 billion (22% of the total) in 2010 to **over $1 trillion, or 44%, by 2025.** By the end of last year, **about 55% of private-credit funds** had extended a loan to a technology borrower. The sample is PitchBook, US borrowers, and a wide definition of “tech” — software, TMT, AI, fintech, climate tech and adjacent verticals. It is not a census of AI data-centre paper. It is still the official map of a sleeve that most investment committees still file under “other credit.”

Two numbers inside the paper matter more than the headline share.

**The first is quality.** Among technology borrowers in the post-2020 cohort, the proportion with **negative EBITDA rose from 23% to 46%.** Nearly half the new borrowers do not generate operating earnings. Among the *profitable* technology borrowers, median leverage **tripled.**

**The second is price.** Over the same period the interquartile range of spreads **compressed from 3.25 percentage points to 1.75.** Lenders were charging a narrower band of prices for a wider dispersion of credit quality. Seniority rose as quality fell: the first-lien share went from **77.6% to 92.2%.** First claim on a cash-flow story is not the same as a safer story.

Put them together and the sentence writes itself. Risk did not leave the system. It changed shape, and it moved into a vehicle that reports quarterly, marks privately, and sits in the column of the asset allocation labelled “diversifying.”

The authors are careful, and so is this edition. The associations with local technology employment are **not causal.** The financial-stability question, they say, **remains open.**

**The counter-case, which is a good one.** Work by Gregor Matvos, Tomasz Piskorski and Amit Seru finds these funds remain roughly **65–80% equity-financed**, against about 10% at US banks. They have not copied bank leverage. A concentrated book funded by equity behaves very differently in a drawdown from one funded by short-term debt: losses hurt the holder, but they do not automatically become somebody else’s funding crisis. Hold both findings. Do not resolve them with a headline.

**Why this is not a private-credit story.** The institutions holding the loans are the institutions holding the equity. A large plan owns the technology complex through its index allocation, which it chose. It owns hyperscaler paper in its credit sleeve, which it chose. It now owns a further claim on the same revenue assumptions through direct-lending funds bought *because they were supposed to behave differently from the equity.* Nobody presented that third sleeve to an investment committee as a technology allocation. It arrived as diversification.

**Why it hurts if you ignore it.** In the next drawdown you will be shown the public-equity tech weight and told the book is “underweight software.” That slide will be true and insufficient. The pain is not a bank-style run. It is a simultaneous mark-down across three columns, a gated vehicle that cannot meet repurchase requests, and a contribution discussion that starts from a number the board did not know it owned. HLEND’s third-quarter repurchase requests ran at roughly **11.5% of shares against a 5% cap** — the fifth consecutive quarter at roughly twice the cap. A repurchase request is not a completed redemption. Repeated proration is a gate working as designed. It is also the liquidity-planning fact.

**The vitamin.** *Look-through.* Before the next IC, ask for one page that adds: listed technology equity + investment-grade hyperscaler credit + technology borrowers inside private credit + any “real-asset” ticket whose offtaker is a GPU tenant. If the four lines share one customer, one capex cycle and one multiple, they are not four risks.

**The term to steal:** **spread IQR.** When the middle 50% of prices get closer together while borrower quality gets further apart, the market is telling you it has stopped discriminating. That is a credit skill, not a macro view.

# Europe has the savings. It does not have the market.

The most useful sentence of the day was spoken in Vienna.

Christine Lagarde addressed *Hofburg im Dialog* on 14 September under the title “A new age of capital: growth, sovereignty and AI.” Her historical frame was the *Gründerzeit*: European savings financed American railroads, and after 1873 the growth stayed in the United States.

Then she put current numbers on the table. Euro-area households save about **€1.4 trillion a year** and hold about **€440 billion in US technology firms.** Europe hosts about **5%** of the world’s AI computing capacity; the United States hosts roughly **three-quarters.** Closing Europe’s data-centre gap over a decade could cost **as much as €600 billion** including chips — an upper bound she flagged herself. Existing budgets and EU instruments leave **more than €100 billion a year** of the public share of strategic investment uncovered. US hyperscalers issued **more than $100 billion of bonds** last year, most of it long-dated, and now account for close to **a tenth of new euro non-financial corporate issuance.** The workforce shrinks by **more than a million people a year for the next twenty-five years.** AI, if adopted quickly, could lift the *level* of productivity by up to **4% over a decade.**

**The underappreciated point is that the €440 billion is not a patriotism statistic. It is a redemption channel.** Lagarde said the holdings sit mostly in funds. Funds can be sold. A US technology drawdown is therefore a European household event before it is a European policy event — and the selling would arrive precisely when a European build-out needed equity most.

Do not write, and do not think, that European pensions are forced sellers of US tech. The channel is redemptions into a falling market, not a statutory dump.

**Now put Vienna next to Basel.** Lagarde says Europe lacks a market that can carry multi-year losses at the scale AI infrastructure requires. The BIS, publishing the same morning, describes exactly the market the United States built to do that job — and shows that nearly half its new technology borrowers have no operating earnings. Europe is not missing a virtue. It is missing an instrument, and the instrument it is missing has just been measured, by the central banks’ central bank, at its most permissive.

**The counter-case.** Euro-area firms will devote around **10%** of total investment to AI this year, and AI-related borrowing was about **a quarter** of first-quarter growth in credit to euro-area firms; more than half of euro-area workers now use AI at work, a doubling in two years. Adoption is real. The €600bn is an upper bound covering a wider gap than GPU-dense facilities. Capital-markets union has been the official answer to this diagnosis for a decade. Naming AI as the catalyst does not enact the legislation.

**Why it hurts if you ignore it.** If you are a European owner, a US tech drawdown hits the household, the sponsor and the portfolio together — and it hits just as Brussels asks you to fund a domestic build. If you are a US or Canadian owner, European “capital coming home” is not a speech. It is a potential seller in the same names you hold in size.

**The vitamin.** Separate **stock** (€440bn already in US tech) from **flow** (€1.4tn saved each year). Policy can redirect a flow. A stock can only be sold. Watch for a large European pension or insurer announcing an AI-infrastructure equity or offtake sleeve. That is the confirm. Another communiqué is not.

**The term to steal:** **redemption channel.** A holding is not a commitment. It is an option the other side of the market can exercise against you.

# Toronto, thirty-six hours
## *The shop window is open. The book is not.*

> Three prime ministers, two parties, one telecom and a prairie government, Monday afternoon in Toronto. The gala that evening was closed to the public and the press. This lineup was not.
> *Nathan Denette / The Canadian Press*

Mark Carney’s first Canada Investment Summit is a two-day, invitation-only convening in Toronto on 14–15 September, co-hosted with CPP Investments and PSP Investments. The official purpose is to catalyse **C$1 trillion** of total investment over five years. The official arithmetic, unchanged since the 17 April announcement, is that about **C$280 billion** of government capital and incentives is “expected to enable” the rest.

**That is enable language. It is not a close.**

Carney’s own line at Sunday’s welcome reception was the honest one: funds managing **“over C$120 trillion”** had come to “peer into our shop window.” A government source told Reuters the same day that major deals could take **twelve to eighteen months.** Industry minister Mélanie Joly told CBC to expect “good deals and MOUs” with impacts in **three months to a year.**

That is the official conversion clock, from the hosts themselves. Anything faster is a rumour until there is a term sheet.

And the C$120 trillion is not a Canadian investment budget. Those assets belong to thousands of clients and mandates across dozens of jurisdictions. It is the size of the room, not the size of the cheque. Print the C. Several wires dropped it.

**Why this summit, this week.** Selected US **50% duties** on steel, aluminium, dairy, furniture and paper take effect **Tuesday, while the room meets.** Ottawa is not hosting a festival of capital. It is trying to thicken a home market while the traditional one is being repriced by tariff.

### Who was actually in the room

Confirmed Monday, from PMO advisories and the PMO’s own readout of six bilateral meetings:

- **Larry Fink** — chairman and chief executive, BlackRock
- **Jonathan Gray** — president and chief operating officer, Blackstone
- **Shemara Wikramanayake** — chief executive, Macquarie
- **Dilhan Pillay** — chief executive, Temasek
- **Sheikh Saoud Salem Abdulaziz Al-Sabah** — board member and managing director, Kuwait Investment Authority

AP separately lists Apollo’s Marc Rowan and KKR co-chief executive Joseph Bae among the guests.

**Fink and Gray lead different firms, in different capacities.** BlackRock is the world’s largest indexer, the owner of the operating system a great many of the other people in the room already pay for, and a growing private-markets platform. Blackstone is a sponsor: it buys, leverages, operates and exits. Temasek and KIA are principals — they can write a cheque that is their own. APG’s Annette Mosman, on Tuesday’s foreign-allocator panel with Fink, Gray and Pillay, is a fiduciary for Dutch pension money. Enthusiasm does not identify the capacity in which a firm would act. A manager may appear as a lender, an equity sponsor, an operating partner, an arranger — or a competitor for the same asset.

On the Tuesday agenda, and therefore in town: John Graham after lunch; energy (Ontario Power Generation, Suncor, TC Energy); defence and manufacturing (CAE, Telesat, Linamar, Bombardier); sessions on AI and critical minerals. A concierge service matches delegates for closed-door conversations. That is the part of the summit that will never be on camera, and it is where any actual transaction begins.

Reported as attending or expected — not all confirmed on site: Saudi PIF, ADNOC/XRG, Mubadala, NBIM, Hong Kong Monetary Authority, IFM Investors, China Investment Corporation, CICC, Berkshire Hathaway, JPMorgan, Barclays. Chinese attendance is the political story inside the room. PMO says the Investment Canada Act and the national-security screen apply as they would to anyone. That is a process answer, not a deal.

IMCO’s Bert Clark publicly confirmed joining. IMCO holds roughly **30% of assets in Canada** and states an intention to maintain that proportion as assets grow. An existing allocation maintained is not new summit money.

The premiers arrived with pitch books. Doug Ford: **15 unnamed Ontario projects** — the list has not been published. Danielle Smith brought **34 Alberta projects.** Wab Kinew, at a side forum, waived Manitoba retail sales tax on Port of Churchill Plus capital expenditure.

The Gulf is the strategic sub-plot. The UAE has already committed **US$50 billion** to Canada on a separate track; XRG has been examining LNG Canada Phase 2. The summit is the show-me-the-money test of that courtship, not the courtship itself.

Stephen Harper, now chair of AIMCo, delivers the Tuesday close. Jean Chrétien stood on the Monday stage. The bipartisan signal is not accidental: **Harper appointed Carney governor of the Bank of Canada in 2008.**

**The one instrument that changes a decision process today is the CRA queue.** A C$1bn-plus structure can now obtain a binding answer on tax treatment *before* capital moves. That is genuine friction removal. It is not a tax cut, not a new ruling product, and not a statutory deadline.

**One governance point almost everyone will miss.** Canada Growth Fund is managed by a PSP subsidiary, while PSP also manages pension assets. Do not attribute a public-policy investment to a pension portfolio because the manager’s name is familiar.

**And one more, which a WSJ reader will ask.** Mark Carney was Brookfield’s board chair and head of transition investing before he sought public office. The Globe reports that discussions between CPP Investments and Brookfield predated Ottawa’s announcement of the summit. That is the conflict-of-role fact. It does not settle whether the vehicle is a good one. It does mean the host of the shop window used to chair one of the two firms now offering to stock it.

### The Maple Fund is a co-underwrite, not a fund

Look at the structure again. A fund has a manager, a fee, a carry, and investors who can decline to re-up. This has two principals, 50/50, tickets of at least C$5bn, and — as reported — **no fees and no carry between the partners.** Other investors may partner case by case.

That is a **co-underwrite.** The discipline that normally arrives through fee pressure has to arrive from somewhere else: independent underwriting by each partner, a strategic-sector screen that has not been published, and the first named asset’s unlevered entry yield. The Globe’s own headline still carries “sources say.” Until a first-party release is attached, that is the language.

**Do not confuse it with Maple Infrastructure Trust**, a completed 11 September transaction in **India** — seven toll roads, Macquarie’s MAIF4 and La Caisse. Unrelated. Any copy that says “Maple” without a qualifier is a defect.

### The Bell headline needs more than a number

Three official rooms produced three official numbers and **one legal instrument.**

Bell’s own newswire, 14 September: a **non-binding memorandum of understanding** with Saskatchewan for phased development of **up to 900 megawatts** of additional capacity, “creating a path to a 1.2 gigawatt Canadian AI infrastructure hub.” At full build-out, total capital “including data centre infrastructure, tenant compute and related power generation could exceed $50 billion.” Development “would proceed in phases as customer commitments are secured.” Forward-looking. No assurance the expansion will occur.

ISED, same day: “total capital investment of **up to $52.5 billion**”; “4,500 high-quality jobs”; a national exploration envelope of up to six gigawatts.

Saskatchewan: the original **300 MW** stays on a SaskPower dedicated industrial feed; **the extra 900 MW is Bring-Your-Own-Power natural gas**, paid for by Bell or a third-party operator.

**Now the arithmetic nobody is doing.** The existing 300 MW hall in the Rural Municipality of Sherwood, announced 16 March 2026, is under construction. Bell’s own capital expenditure on that hall is about **C$1.7 billion.** Capacity is pre-sold to US neoclouds **CoreWeave and Cerebras.** At C$1.7bn per hall, Bell’s own build of additional facilities sits on a path closer to **C$5 billion than to C$50 billion.** The C$50–52.5bn stack is **infrastructure plus tenant GPUs plus the gas plant.**

Do not write “Bell is investing $52.5 billion.” Write: non-binding MOU; path to 1.2 GW; up to C$52.5bn all-in including tenant compute and off-grid gas; Bell’s own cheque is phased multi-year capex against customer offtake.

**The job figures do not reconcile.** ISED says 4,500. Saskatchewan says up to 500 permanent operations, about 100 management, an estimated 3,000 in security, logistics and maintenance. Bell says 800–1,200 construction and up to 600 permanent. Range them. Do not pick 4,500.

**The embedded-gas flag, stated plainly.** The additional 900 MW is partner-developed natural-gas generation, behind the meter. For any infrastructure or climate mandate, that is an **explicit embedded gas exposure inside a project marketed as digital sovereignty.** Evaluate the generation’s fuel cost, emissions, future policy treatment and useful life **separately** from the building and separately from the computing equipment. A digital-sovereignty label does not determine a carbon profile.

This is where the summit and the BIS lead meet. Long-duration demand can justify very large investments. It can also be counted more than once by different capital providers — the developer counts the campus, the lender counts the facility, the tenant counts the compute. Following the legal payer is as useful in Saskatchewan as in any global infrastructure book.

### The prospectus — 167 lines, and the disclaimer that matters

A 66-page pitchbook of **167 projects** circulated to attendees. PMO told BetaKit it did not share a prospectus with the media. The deck’s own disclaimer: **listing is not an endorsement.**

More than a third of the book is minerals and mining. The digital chapter lists **96 data centres “in development.”** Energy tickets circulating: a West Coast oil pipeline around **C$35bn**; **Ksi Lisims LNG** around **C$28.5bn**; **Nova Scotia Wind West** around **C$44bn / 5 GW.** Port of Churchill is printed at **US$57bn (p.41 — US dollars).** It has also circulated as C$57bn and C$70–80bn. There is no FID. Do not pick one number, and do not convert one into the other silently.

Published capex totals for the book do not agree with each other. The Logic puts it above **US$425 billion**; other tallies run higher. Do not publish a single sum. Say: 167 projects, tickets from tens of millions to tens of billions, conventional energy at the front, **and listing is not a close.**

A practical way to sort the pipeline, which is worth more to a reader than the total:

| Group | What it is | What the missing counterparty usually is |
|---|---|---|
| **1** | Operating assets and expansions | The **seller’s valuation** |
| **2** | Permitted but unfunded projects | A **customer or offtake**, not another investor |
| **3** | Technical or early-development concepts | An **engineering milestone** |

An institution can invest in all three. It must not apply the same leverage, liquidity or return expectations to them. Calling every obstacle “a shortage of capital” makes it harder to identify the intervention that would actually move the project.

Ksi Lisims offtake with Germany’s SEFE (1 mtpa, May) and Uniper (2 mtpa, July) predates the summit. Do not relaunch those cargoes as a Tuesday announcement.

It is not on the summit agenda. A room being asked to underwrite twenty-year Canadian assets should be told that case exists.

**Indigenous participation is not a single view.** Sean Willy of Des Nedhe Group and Kristopher Wojtecki of Starlight Infrastructure Solutions argue for Indigenous ownership as a foundation of investible projects. PBI-Canada separately reports opposition from hereditary leaders to major project proposals. These must not be collapsed. An investor needs the specific rights holders attached to **each asset.** Ownership by one group does not answer every consent question affecting a project.

**The universal-owner test on privatisation.** Unifor’s Aviation Council publicly opposed airport privatisation at the summit. That establishes the union’s position; it does not establish that an airport sale has been agreed. Higher asset revenue can come from productive investment **or from transferring costs to users.** For an owner of the whole economy those are not economically equivalent.

### Allocator lens

**1 · Direct investment exposure.** A Canadian allocation can diversify geography while increasing concentration in familiar global themes — AI demand, power systems, commodities, infrastructure construction. Start with existing positions. Then ask whether the proposed commitment adds an **independent return source**, or merely another claim on a customer, commodity or financing cycle already in the book. Bell AI Fabric is the worked example: an allocator that already owns the hyperscaler equity, the technology private credit and the gas midstream can buy a “Canadian diversifier” that is a fourth claim on one revenue assumption.

**2 · Sponsor, contributions and liabilities.** A Canadian beneficiary is exposed to the domestic economy through employment, housing and future public services as well as retirement assets. A stronger productive economy helps sponsors and contributions. Greater domestic investment concentrates risk if one shock hits portfolio assets, sponsor finances and household purchasing power together. An overseas owner may see Canada as diversification; a Canadian owner must account for additional home-country exposure.

**3 · The wider system.** If the public sector absorbs demand risk, that fact must be visible in the return discussion.

**The investment-committee question.** Before this committee treats any summit number as a 2026–27 pacing input: which of the three systems is our ticket actually in — political home-market thickening, platform recycling, or buying the manager rather than the prospectus — and what is the unlevered entry yield on the first named asset, as opposed to the press-release total?

**The scorecard worth maintaining.** By year-end: **term sheet · power offtake · a ruling inside the new CRA queue** on any of the 167 lines. Enable is still not close. The date on which you have to be right has not moved.

**Why it hurts if you ignore it.** You will be asked, in a year, why the C$1 trillion did not arrive. The honest answer is that most of what was waved on 14–15 September was never a cheque. The painful answer is that you treated capacity as capital, an MOU as a close, and a prospectus as a pipeline — and then paced a real portfolio against a political total.

**The vitamin.** Three columns, kept honestly: **financing made available / financing contracted / money actually drawn.** Plus one word: **stage.** Intention, capacity, incentive, MOU, objective, close. If a slide cannot say which, it is not a slide. It is a brochure.

**The terms to steal:** **enable vs close**; **co-underwrite**; **BYOP**; **queue jump.**

# CalPERS returned 14.8%. Its five-year is 6.83%. The board has to choose today.

CalPERS posted a preliminary net return of **14.8%** for the twelve months to 30 June 2026 — above the prior year’s 11.6% and well above the **6.8%** assumed rate. The Public Employees’ Retirement Fund held **US$637.1 billion**; funded status rose to **85%** from 79%. Public equity **24.1%**; private equity **17.0%.** (Private-market valuations lag one quarter, as of 31 March 2026.)

Now the number almost nobody printed. **The preliminary five-year annualised return is 6.83%.** The assumed rate is 6.8%.

**Three basis points.** After a 24.1% year in public equity and a 17% year in private equity, the five-year record clears the discount rate by three hundredths of a percentage point. The ten-year is **8.57%.** The twenty-year is **6.81% — below the assumption.**

Under the Funding Risk Mitigation Policy, a year like this one puts an item on the board agenda. It is an option rather than a mechanism because **the automatic trigger was removed in April 2024.** Before that change, a sufficiently strong year stepped the rate down by formula. The current rule is more precise than “a return above the discount rate.” A discussion is required when the fiscal-year return exceeds the assumed rate **by 2.0 percentage points or more.** At 6.8%, that hurdle is **8.8%.** Fourteen-point-eight clears it. The Finance and Administration Committee takes the item **today at 09:30 Pacific.** The full Board sits **16 September** and can take a committee recommendation. A committee item is not a board decision, and this edition does not record one.

**State the direction, because it is routinely reversed: lowering the discount rate raises the measured liability.** A board that responds to a 14.8% year by cutting its rate converts an investment gain into a larger reported obligation and, in time, higher employer contributions. It is not a victory lap. It is the purchase of a more honest liability with part of this year’s return.

**The corporate mirror.** The Milliman 100 Pension Funding Index (31 August data): the corporate sample is funded at **112.2%**, a surplus of **US$141 billion** on **US$1.158 trillion** of projected benefit obligation, at a **6.0%** discount rate. Zorast Wadia called it a **25-year high.** Corporate defined benefit is in surplus and discounting at 6.0%. A large public plan is deciding whether it can still justify 6.8% after a five-year run of 6.83%. The two halves of the American DB system are moving in opposite directions, and the discount rate is the hinge.

A price window is not a volume window. The June buyout index sits at about **99.6%** of accounting liabilities — pension risk transfer is cheap. Yet **Q1 2026 buyout volume was US$3.8 billion, down 47% year on year.** Sponsors can afford to transfer and are not transferring. Do not mix the corporate index with the public-plan PPFI.

Tomorrow, **16 September**, the FOMC releases its statement, vote and Summary of Economic Projections. That is a policy rate. CalPERS’ 6.8% is a liability assumption. They are not the same object. They will be discussed in the same week as if they were.

**Why it hurts if you ignore it.** If the board cuts the rate, employer contribution rates rise just as cities and schools are being told the fund “had a great year.” If the board does not cut, and the next decade looks like the last twenty rather than the last ten, the honesty was deferred, not avoided. Either way, a communications problem becomes a funding problem if the five-year and twenty-year are not on the same slide as the 14.8%.

**The vitamin.** Always print **1-year / 5-year / 10-year / 20-year against the assumed rate.** A single year is a press release. The four-horizon strip is the job.

**The term to steal:** **discount rate.** It is not a return target you celebrate. It is the interest rate used to measure a promise. Lower it, and the promise gets more expensive today so that it is more likely to be payable later.

# The long horizon — the insurer is leaving two rooms at once

Universal owners have spent a decade being told climate risk reaches them through asset prices. It is reaching them faster through **contract terms** — and this month in two rooms at once.

**Room one is climate property.** The California FAIR Plan, the state’s insurer of last resort, has grown from roughly **127,000 policies in 2018 to more than 668,000 at the end of 2025.** Swiss Re’s position is that a quiet catastrophe year is luck, not trend, with insured losses growing **5–7% in real terms** annually. Fitch has described reinsurance as being in a “controlled descent.” 2026 has produced **no Atlantic hurricane through mid-September** — not a reprieve, an unpriced option expiring.

**Room two is artificial intelligence.** On 4 September, CSIS published an analysis that takes as its starting point a 23 April report in *The Information*: US state insurance commissioners have approved **more than 80%** of carrier requests to **exclude AI-related damages** from corporate policies. EIOPA has asked for a European Observatory on Insurability. CSIS did not count the filings. It asked what it means when the private market withdraws.

**Now join them to the lead — as an argument, not as a finding of the BIS.** If a large and rising share of private credit is lent against technology cash flows, and carriers are simultaneously excluding AI-related damages from the policies held by operating companies generating those cash flows, then **the hedge is being withdrawn from the exposure at the same moment the exposure concentrates.** Whether any private-credit lender has repriced for that is not established. January 2027 renewals will tell.

**And the payor is moving.** On 31 August, Chief Judge Brenda Sannes of the Northern District of New York held that New York’s Climate Change Superfund Act — a **US$75 billion** statutory cost-recovery programme against fossil-fuel producers — is **preempted by the Clean Air Act.** Preemption does not retire the cost. It changes who pays. If the statutory channel closes, adaptation spending stays with states, cities, insurers and — through municipal credit, utility regulation and physical-asset insurance — with the diversified owner of everything those balance sheets touch. Do not describe the Act as finally enjoined until the order issues. Vermont’s analogue remains live. The Supreme Court hears *Suncor Energy Inc. v. Board of County Commissioners of Boulder County* on **5 October.**

The EPA on 14 September **finalised** a partial repeal of the 2024 carbon standards for coal- and gas-fired plants, and separately **proposed** rescinding remaining greenhouse-gas requirements. Final agency action and a proposal are not the same thing. Bell AI Fabric’s 900 MW of behind-the-meter gas is being built into exactly this regulatory dispersion.

**Why it hurts if you ignore it.** An uninsured loss does not disappear. It migrates — into the credit, the municipal, the utility, the residual market, the tax bill. A universal owner pays at least one of those.

**The vitamin.** Read the **exclusion**, not the sustainability report. Ask the private-credit manager whether January wordings still assume an insurable AI event. Ask the real-asset manager whether the campus is on a residual-market property form.

**The term to steal:** **insurability withdrawal.** Not “climate risk.” Not “AI risk.” The contract is leaving.

# Geopolitics — one section, sized as a section

On the Saudi primary record, as supplied: SPA w2674017 (11 September), quoting the Energy Ministry — attacks on the morning of Thursday 10 September in the Riyadh and Madinah regions; the East–West pipeline “was shut down as a precautionary measure.” SPA N2674116 (12 September), the MFA — drones “launched from Iraq”; “some damage that is currently being addressed”; at Iraq’s request Saudi Arabia “opted not to respond at this stage.” Iran denied responsibility on Monday.

No official duration, throughput or stock figure has been published. The ~**7 mb/d** figure is what SPA used on **12 April 2026** when it announced full pumping capacity restored after an earlier strike. It is a restoration-notice nameplate, not observed 2026 throughput. Wartime flow is **2.6–4.0 mb/d (Rystad).** Capital Economics’ “as much as 4% of world supply” is **conditional, not observed.** Do not write that 4% of global supply is offline.

Brent settled at **$105.68** on 14 September, having traded near $110 intraday. Intraday extremes differ across wires; the settle is used. No new Lloyd’s Joint War Committee circular — **JWLA-034 remains 29 July 2026.**

Oman postponed the Salalah ministerial between Gulf states and Iran. No new date.

Two accounts that must not be reconciled in a sentence: the US Energy Secretary’s claim that Hormuz flows are back to two-thirds sits against weekend ship-tracking in the single digits. Report both, attribute both, resolve neither.

**The transmission is not the Brent print.** It is middle distillates, freight and war-risk cover — which reach inflation breakevens, liability discount rates, and tanker and insurance books. For Gulf sovereign funds the combination is the awkward one: **a high price with reduced liftings is a cash-flow squeeze, not a windfall**, for a state funding domestic programmes out of volumes.

Iran’s Persian Gulf Strait Authority has published a list described as covering **77 vessels.** The announcement is confirmed via Reuters. The vessel identities, the alleged violations, the legal reach and any insurer response are not. No detention has been documented.

**Why it hurts if you ignore it.** You will be asked about the oil price. The book will be hit through diesel, freight, war-risk premia, and the discount rate on a long-dated liability — and, for anyone with Gulf SWF co-investors, through a partner whose cash-flow is volume, not price.

**The vitamin.** **Nameplate is not flow.** Restoration language is not a throughput print. JWLA-034 is the insurance fact until there is a new circular.

# The Allocator

I have been handed a slide called “Canada — Diversification Opportunity.”

There is a map. There is a pipeline of one hundred and sixty-seven projects. There is a very large number at the bottom, and the number has the word “enable” somewhere above it, in a smaller font.

I ask which of the one hundred and sixty-seven we are actually looking at. I am told we are looking at the opportunity.

I ask who the payer is. There is a pause of the kind that tells you the answer is in the room but not on the slide.

A prospectus is a list of things that would like to be investments. It is a perfectly respectable document. It is simply not the same document as a term sheet.

I have asked for the second one.

# The question, for the next investment committee

Not “what is our technology exposure.” You will be shown the equity number and it will be wrong.

Ask instead: **which bodies outside this room can change the composition of this portfolio without our consent — and what is the most recent date on which we measured that?**

A central bank can do it with a chart. A government can do it with a calendar. And an asset owner on the other side of the world can do it to your business by grading how you vote.
