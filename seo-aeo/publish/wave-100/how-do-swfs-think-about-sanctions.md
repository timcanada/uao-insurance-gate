# How do sovereign wealth funds think about sanctions?

A sovereign wealth fund is not exempt from sanctions because it is a ministry book. The Santiago Principles say investment decisions should sit on economic and financial grounds, and the official commentary to GAPP 19.1 names legally binding international sanctions as a reason some funds exclude names. US court immunity under the FSIA is a different statute from OFAC. The 50 percent ownership rule still applies. This page is not a ranking and not sanctions advice.

## Immunity is not an OFAC licence

The International Forum of Sovereign Wealth Funds’ [Santiago Principles](https://www.ifswf.org/santiago-principles) (24 Generally Accepted Principles and Practices, October 2008; voluntary; subordinate to local law) already write the investment test:

1. **GAPP 19** — the SWF’s investment decisions should aim to maximize risk-adjusted financial returns in a manner consistent with its investment policy, **based on economic and financial grounds**.
2. **GAPP 19.1** — if investment decisions are subject to other than economic and financial considerations, those reasons should be clearly set out in the investment policy and **publicly disclosed**. The official commentary lists **legally binding international sanctions** (and social, ethical, or religious screens) as the kind of non-financial exclusion a fund must disclose.
3. One of the Santiago objectives is that SWFs **comply with all applicable regulatory and disclosure requirements** in the countries in which they invest.

A US courtroom is a different statute. [28 U.S.C. § 1604](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/28/1604) says a foreign state is immune from the jurisdiction of US courts except as provided in sections 1605 to 1607. [28 U.S.C. § 1605(a)(2)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/28/1605) is the **commercial activity** exception — an action based on a commercial activity carried on in the United States by the foreign state, or an act performed in the United States in connection with a commercial activity of the foreign state elsewhere, or an act outside the United States in connection with such a commercial activity that causes a direct effect in the United States.

Immunity from a civil suit is not an OFAC exemption. OFAC’s [50 Percent Rule](https://ofac.treasury.gov/faqs/399) (**13 August 2014**) still treats an entity owned **50 percent or more in the aggregate** by blocked persons as itself blocked, even if it is not on the SDN list. A sovereign balance sheet does not remove that arithmetic.
