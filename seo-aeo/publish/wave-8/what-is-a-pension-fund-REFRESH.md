# What Is a Pension Fund?

A pension fund is a pool of capital invested to pay retirement benefits to **named members**. Workers and employers contribute during working life; the fund invests over decades; benefits are paid in retirement. It is an asset owner: it sits with the liability. It is **not** a sovereign wealth fund, even when the sponsor is a government. It is also **not** an insurer general account — [how insurers invest differently from pensions](/how-do-insurers-invest-differently-from-pensions/). Public plans that publish the book sit on [how public pensions publish holdings](/how-do-public-pensions-publish-holdings/). Japan’s [GPIF](/gpif-japan-explained/) is the largest dedicated public pension (**¥293,643.7 billion** of investment assets at 31 March 2026). This is not investment advice.

## What it is

A pension fund collects contributions, invests them, and pays benefits. The legal wrappers differ — trust, corporation, government account — but the economic job does not. Someone is owed a retirement income. The fund exists so that promise (or that DC balance) is not an unfunded claim on next year’s budget.

Two designs do most of the work:

- **Defined benefit.** The plan promises a formula. The fund (and the sponsor) carry investment and longevity risk. Funded status — assets versus the actuarial obligation — is the health number.
- **Defined contribution.** The member owns an account. The contribution is the promise. Investment risk sits with the member unless the plan adds a guarantee.

[Public pension funds](/public-pension-funds/) are the large government-sponsored DB books. Corporate plans, industry-wide multi-employer plans, and sovereign *pension reserve* funds sit on the same liability idea with different politics.

## Pension fund vs the things people confuse it with

| | Pension fund | Sovereign wealth fund | Asset manager |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Who is owed | Members | Often no named beneficiary | The client |
| Residual risk | The plan / sponsor / member | The state | The client (the firm earns a fee) |
| Typical score | Funded status + long-horizon return | Mandate return or NPV | Relative return vs a benchmark |
| Can it be a universal owner? | Yes, at scale | Yes, at scale | No — it is the agent |

IFSWF’s Santiago definition **excludes** public pension funds because they are ultimately owned by the underlying policyholders. See [Santiago Principles](/santiago-principles/).

## How they invest

The book has to last as long as the members. That is why large pensions hold public equity and bonds *and*, increasingly, private equity, real estate, infrastructure, and private credit. The Canadian model ([CPP Investments](/cpp-investments-explained/), [OTPP](/ontario-teachers-pension-plan-explained/), [PSP](/psp-investments/)) built large in-house teams. GPIF stayed four liquid sleeves near 25% each, with alternatives capped at 5%. Both are pension designs. Neither is a hedge fund.

A [total portfolio approach](/total-portfolio-approach/) is how some of these desks now allocate — one fund-level risk budget instead of a pile of asset-class silos. CalPERS said it launched that approach in July 2026.

## Frequently asked questions

**What is the largest pension fund in the world?**  
On a beneficiary-pension definition: **GPIF**. Norway’s GPFG is larger as an *asset owner* and is not a pension.

**How much do the largest pensions control?**  
TAI / WTW top-300 work: about **US$24.4 trillion at end-2024**. Confirm the vintage before a slide.

**Is a sovereign wealth fund a pension fund?**  
No. Some SWFs pre-fund future public pensions. That still does not make GPFG, [CIC](/china-investment-corporation-cic-explained/), or [PIF](/public-investment-fund-pif/) a pension.
